The Knob, Explained Simply

Memory Box · Magnavox radio · what goes where, and why

The whole idea in one sentence: the old radio knob used to turn a metal rod that was bolted to the radio's guts. You threw the guts away. So now you build a tiny shelf inside the cabinet, bolt a new electronic knob to that shelf, and poke its rod out through the same hole the old one used.

1What we're building, from the side

Imagine slicing the radio in half like a sandwich and looking at the cut edge. Your face is on the left. The inside of the cabinet is on the right.

Left to right: knob → tiny air gap → radio face → nut → bracket → encoder → wood shelf.

Why each piece exists

  • Wood strip. The encoder needs something solid to hold onto. The radio face alone is too thin and will eventually wobble loose. So you glue a stick of wood across the inside of the cabinet — like a towel bar — and everything hangs off that.
  • Plastic bracket. You can't screw an encoder to a wood strip directly. So the encoder gets bolted to a flat plastic square first, and the plastic square gets screwed to the wood.
  • Washers. Stacking washers behind the bracket slides it forward or backward a millimeter at a time — that's how you get the knob to stick out exactly as far as it used to.
  • 2mm air gap. If the knob touches the face, it scrapes and the push-button can't push. Leave it a hair of daylight.

2The wires — only five of them

Five wires go from the little encoder board to the Pi's row of pins. That's it. No soldering required to test it, no resistors, no circuit board.

How to count to pin 11 without losing your mind

The Pi's pins are two rows of 20. Hold the Pi with the pins along the top edge. Pin 1 is the one closest to the corner, on the row nearer the edge of the board — it usually has a little square solder pad instead of a round one. Odd numbers go down that row, even numbers down the other.

Colored pins are the five you use. Everything else stays empty.

The one mistake that kills the Pi: putting the red wire on pin 2 or pin 4. Those are 5 volts and they will fry the input pins. Pin 1 only. It's the very first pin in the corner.

3Making the old knob fit the new shaft

The new encoder's shaft is 6mm. The hole in your vintage knob is almost certainly some other size or shape. Rather than hunting for an adapter, you mold one.

No measuring, no adapter to order, works no matter how weird the original hole is. And because the encoder doesn't care which way it's pointing, you never have to line the knob up with anything — unlike the old tuner.

4The order to actually do it in

  1. On the bench, nothing mounted. Plug the five wires into the Pi, run the code, turn the encoder with your fingers. Make sure a game changes. Do not mount anything until this works.
  2. Cut the plastic bracket and bolt the encoder to it. Now hold the bracket up behind the radio face by hand and see how far the shaft pokes through.
  3. Cut and glue the wood strip inside the cabinet at the right depth so the knob ends up sitting where it used to.
  4. Screw the bracket to the strip, adding washers until the knob depth looks right and there's a 2mm gap.
  5. Mold the knob onto the shaft.
  6. Only now do the artwork and paint. Every hole is final at this point.
Step 1 is the important one. Get the electronics working on a table where you can see everything before anything is glued inside a cabinet. If something's wrong, you want to find out while it's still five loose wires.

Bracket Cut List

What to cut, what to buy, in what order · Memory Box build #1

Your instincts were right on two of three. The PVC board: yes, perfect. The washers: yes, buy a small pack. The wood shims: no — those are tapered wedges (thin at one end, fat at the other). Used as a spacer they'd tilt the encoder and your knob would sit crooked in the hole. Good news: you don't need wood at all. Cut everything from the PVC board you already have.

1Three pieces. That's the whole bracket.

Total material: a piece of PVC board about 4" × 5". Scrap-sized.

Thickness matters — for the strip only

The encoder's threaded collar is only 5mm long, so the strip it bolts through has to be about ⅛" (3mm). Any thicker and the threads never poke through far enough for the nut to grab.

  • ⅛" board → ideal. Strip straight from it; glue two or three layers together for each block.
  • ¼" or thicker → still fine, but counterbore the encoder hole: drill a shallow ⅜"–½" dish about 3mm deep on the front face, then the 9/32" hole through the middle of it. Blocks can be full thickness — they're only spacers, so they don't care.

See shaft-length-guide.html for the picture of this.

2How the three pieces go together

Two blocks glued to the back of the radio face, strip screwed across them, encoder in the middle.

The blocks sit on either side of the knob hole, about 3 inches apart. Because they're glued right to the back of the face, the whole assembly references the face itself — so the knob depth is predictable instead of guesswork. Short span means even thin PVC won't flex.

The thing that makes this easy: you're molding the knob's hole with epoxy putty, so you decide how deep the knob swallows the shaft. That means you don't have to do any math to make the lengths work out. Build the bracket at whatever thickness is solid, see how much shaft pokes out the front, and mold the knob to take up exactly that much. The shaft length problem solves itself.

Check your bench first — you may already have these

  • PVC board scrap — 4" × 5" is plenty
  • E6000 or 2-part epoxy — for gluing the blocks to the face
  • 9/32" drill bit — this is the one to double-check. The encoder needs a 7mm hole; 9/32" is 7.14mm, a perfect match. ¼" is too small and 5/16" is sloppy. A step bit works too. Add it to the cart if you don't have it (~$4).
  • Female-to-female jumper wires, 5 needed — if you don't have them, grab a pack on the same Amazon order (~$6)
  • PTFE / plumber's tape (for the knob molding release)
Do not buy: the tapered wood shims by the aisle endcap, wood strips, or L-brackets. Tapered shims aren't flat, and you don't need wood — the PVC does everything here. Fewer materials, fewer things to go wrong.

4Cutting PVC board

  • ⅛" – ¼" board: score it deeply with a utility knife against a straightedge, three or four passes, then snap it over a table edge. Like drywall. Clean edge, no dust, no power tools.
  • Thicker: a fine-tooth hand saw or a jigsaw. Go at moderate speed — too fast and the friction melts PVC back together behind the blade.
  • Drilling: back the piece with a scrap so the bit doesn't blow out the far side. Light pressure. Regular twist bits are fine.
  • Edges: a few swipes with sandpaper. Nobody sees these parts anyway.

5Order of operations

  1. Get the encoder working on the bench with five loose wires, before you cut anything.
  2. Cut the strip and the two blocks. Drill the 9/32" hole in the strip's center and the two screw holes at the ends.
  3. Bolt the encoder to the strip. Nut on the front, snug.
  4. Dry-fit with no glue: hold the blocks in place, rest the strip on them, look at how much shaft comes through the face. Add or remove washers.
  5. Glue the blocks to the back of the face. Let cure fully.
  6. Screw the strip on. Mold the knob onto the shaft.
  7. Confirm the knob turns freely, has a 2mm gap, and still clicks when pressed.
  8. Only then — artwork and paint.
